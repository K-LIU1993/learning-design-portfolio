# Decentralised Collaboration and AI-Mediated Practices in a Member-Governed Makerspace

Abstract: Member-governed makerspaces rely on voluntary contribution, but the relationships among governance, informal learning, and emerging technologies remain underexplored. This qualitative case study draws on nine semi-structured interviews in one UK makerspace. Abductive reflexive thematic analysis, informed by commons governance, Self-Determination Theory, and sociomateriality, developed three interpretive findings. Participants described formal rules alongside reputational authority earned through visible contribution. They also described individually pursued projects that became socially meaningful when shared and discussed, a pattern termed “mutual witnessing.” Accounts of AI use and imagined uses favoured bounded assistance while retaining human verification and responsibility. The study shows how members understood the conditions for participation and peer learning in this setting. It offers a case-grounded hypothesis about the place of AI in such practices, without claiming measured learning outcomes or causal effects on the makerspace’s continued operation.

# Introduction

Makerspaces combine shared tools, expertise, and physical space for making and peer learning (Halverson & Sheridan, 2014). Some are governed by members rather than paid managers, so access, maintenance, and the transmission of know-how depend on continuing voluntary contribution (Toombs et al., 2015). These spaces are also sites of informal, collaborative learning: members can make, observe, explain, and revise work in the company of others. Understanding how they remain open for such activity therefore involves more than accounting for tools or rules.

In a member-governed makerspace, authority is negotiated through participation rather than assigned by a conventional managerial hierarchy. AI presents a further question: it may assist with search, prototyping, and routine coordination, but its place in a community built on shared judgement cannot be inferred from its technical capacities alone. Research on AI teammates has largely examined settings in which roles can be allocated by an organisation or task design (Dennis et al., 2023). Less is known about how members of a voluntary learning community describe the uses they would accept, limit, or refuse.

This paper examines a UK makerspace that participants described as active over many years. It asks how participants describe collaborative practices associated with its continued operation (RQ1), and how they describe AI as supporting, complicating, or threatening those practices (RQ2). “Continued operation” refers here to participants’ accounts of community life, not a measured survival outcome. The local case contributes to the ISLS theme “Act local, think global” by asking what becomes of participation and peer learning when a community negotiates the place of an emerging technology.

# Theoretical framework

Three lenses connect the questions that this case brings together: how shared resources are governed, why members continue to participate, and how technologies become part of everyday practice. They guide interpretation rather than supply a fixed coding scheme.

Ostrom’s (1990) commons framework draws attention to locally legitimate boundaries, collective choice, monitoring, sanctions, and conflict resolution. The extension to knowledge commons also makes access, contribution, and stewardship of shared information visible (Hess & Ostrom, 2007). In a makerspace, tools and rooms must be allocated, while sharing expertise also requires members’ finite time and attention. This distinction matters because rules for equipment alone cannot explain how members keep knowledge available to one another.

Self-Determination Theory (SDT) identifies autonomy, competence, and relatedness as needs implicated in sustained voluntary activity (Ryan & Deci, 2017). Han et al. (2017) associated support for these needs with makerspace continuance intention. Their analytical separation need not mean that the needs are experienced separately in practice; balanced satisfaction across them is itself consequential (Sheldon & Niemiec, 2006). The present case therefore considers how choosing a project, developing skill, and being recognised by others may be connected within one activity.

A sociomaterial perspective treats social and material arrangements as mutually shaped in practice (Orlikowski, 2007). Attending to the properties of people and technologies helps specify how a tool enables or constrains particular activities (Leonardi, 2013). For this study, the question is not simply whether members use AI, but how they locate verification, learning, and responsibility when they do.

# Methods

The study used an interpretivist, qualitative single-site case design. Purposive recruitment through posters in the makerspace and a call in its members’ Discord channel yielded nine interviewees: eight current members, including two trustees, and one visitor who toured but did not join. Their involvement ranged from recent membership to fourteen years; interviews lasted approximately 34–107 minutes and were conducted in person or online. The visitor’s account offers a limited view of entry, not an explanation of departure. Interviews addressed coordination, trust, conflict, contribution, knowledge sharing, motivation, and AI use.

The researcher was a member of the makerspace. Reflexive notes and an audit trail recorded the possible influence of that position on recruitment and interpretation. Ethical approval preceded data collection; participants gave written informed consent, and identifying details were pseudonymised. Transcripts and field notes were analysed in MAXQDA using reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2022). Analysis moved abductively between accounts and the three theoretical lenses. The 959 coded excerpts and 85 initial codes were reorganised into three theory-informed themes and one background category, with 15 sub-themes in all. These counts document analytic coverage, not prevalence, saturation, or relative importance.

The findings are a further interpretive step, not simply the names of the coding categories. Comparing the governance sub-themes on collective choice, monitoring, and continuity with participants’ accounts of trustee standing led to the first finding. Reading autonomy, competence, and relatedness alongside accounts of individual projects and entry led to the second. Juxtaposing AI-use, attitude, and sociomaterial sub-themes with accounts of verification and responsibility led to the third. The analysis concerns reported experiences; it does not measure learning gains or the causal effect of AI.

# Findings

The three findings describe patterns and tensions within participants’ accounts: reputational authority beneath formal governance, participation through “mutual witnessing,” and selective AI use. The quotations below are illustrative episodes rather than measures of how widely any view was held.

## Reputational authority beneath formal governance

Participants described rules for membership and tools, an elected trustee board, teams responsible for equipment, and ways of responding to misuse. P1, a long-standing member, used the term “mana” for a form of standing that did not depend on office: “the inevitable quality of a charismatic person who is trusted as a leader of their community without being a bully, without having an inherited position of power.” The term is retained as P1’s own description of socially earned authority; no wider cultural meaning is inferred. He also called trustees “a group of leaders who are elected through a voting mechanism on a cycle.” Read together, these remarks suggest that an election can formalise trust built through sustained, visible contribution. Yet participants did not agree on the language of leadership. P9 said “trustees are not leaders,” and P2 said “there’s no leaders, we’re all members.” Their disagreement suggests that the absence of a formal hierarchy did not settle how members recognised authority.

P1 also called trusteeship “a very thankless task,” saying trustees “don’t get power, you don’t get any rewards.” These remarks place unpaid governance within a relationship of recognition and responsibility. P2 said that members had “ways of regulating” and could often resolve disputes as adults, but both P1 and P2 named trustees as the people to contact when an issue needed attention. Repeated misuse could lead to a ban. The juxtaposition is important: ordinary cooperation did not remove the need for an accountable point of contact. Formal procedures made that accountability possible, while participants’ trust in particular people helped make those procedures workable.

## Sustained participation through mutual witnessing

Participants did not always make things together. P4 described work on robotics: “I mainly work myself, like with the robots… they do their own robots, their robots… we don’t exchange…but yeah, we share our ideas.” The hesitation between “we don’t exchange” and “we share our ideas” is revealing. His projects were individually directed, yet their progress could still be noticed and discussed. This paper calls that pattern *mutual witnessing*: members make separate work visible to one another, creating occasions for explanation, feedback, and recognition without requiring a single joint artefact. Wiki entries, Discord conversations, and informal demonstrations offered additional ways for project knowledge to circulate. P3 linked the Wiki and Discord with the distribution of information and the building of trust. These accounts associate choosing work with autonomy, developing and explaining it with competence, and being recognised with relatedness. Visibility created opportunities for peer learning; the interviews did not show how much any individual learned.

Access to those occasions was uneven. P9 described the space as “a little bit cliquey…for a totally new [member].” He spent “the first year…quite a lot of time on the Discord just chatting to people” and came in person after getting to know some members online. Even then, he felt he needed a project to justify being in the space. His account shows that an open membership rule need not make the social route into making obvious; online conversation served as a tentative bridge. P5, who toured but did not join, cited cost and distance. Her account limits what can be inferred about those who never enter: practices that help current members remain involved cannot by themselves remove the threshold of entry.

## Selective absorption of AI

Participants described AI use for search, coding or data work, and early prototyping, while qualifying what could be trusted. P4 said that he used AI to “quickly design the prototype” and then revised it “to make it exactly what I want,” because “you need strong background knowledge to correct AI’s mistakes.” P2 similarly emphasised checking information and remaining “the human asking the right question.” P8 called AI “exciting” while saying he was “not excited about everything to do with AI.” These accounts do not imply rejection of AI; they locate responsibility for interpretation and correction with the member, even where initial assistance is welcome.

P1 offered a sharper case. An assistant asked whether he wanted a summary of an important email. He replied that “if an email is important enough to require a summary, it is important enough for me to read it thoroughly and comprehend it myself.” In this episode, accuracy was not the only issue: his objection concerned the attention required to understand information for which he might be responsible. Asked to imagine AI in the space, P4 suggested an information screen, P7 a help desk, and P5 a robot; P1 instead imagined “a big bin outside.” These different proposals indicate possibilities and reservations, not a settled community policy. Together with the reported uses, they suggest an interest in assistance that remains open to human checking and judgement.

# Discussion and implications

This case brings commons governance and SDT into conversation with an account of learning through participation. Rules and trustees structured access to shared resources, while visible contribution helped some members recognise whose judgement they trusted. The accounts of individual projects suggest that autonomy, competence, and relatedness can be experienced through the same act of making visible. This reading extends Han et al.’s (2017) model as a hypothesis about practice, not a test or refutation of its statistical relationships. It also resonates with constructionist attention to shareable artefacts (Holbert et al., 2020) and learning through participation (Wenger, 1998). P9’s account adds a qualification: visibility matters differently to a newcomer who is still learning how to enter the room. What the interviews establish is how members described opportunities to explain and exchange work; they do not demonstrate learning outcomes.

The AI accounts suggest a related question for the governance of knowledge. If a member uses a tool to find, summarise, or generate information, who checks it, and what opportunities remain to build a shared understanding? P1’s email episode makes this question concrete but cannot show that AI depleted collective knowledge. For makerspaces considering AI, the case suggests discussing the tasks for which help is useful, the verification each task requires, and who remains accountable. Such decisions would be part of the community’s existing practices of trust and collective choice, rather than a conclusion that any particular tool should be adopted or excluded.

The contribution is bounded by the sample and design. Eight interviewees were current members, and only one had toured without joining; those who left or never visited are largely absent. The researcher’s membership shaped access and interpretation despite reflexive documentation. P1 supplied many coded excerpts, while the “paradox of AI” comes from one episode. Interviews did not follow AI use or learning over time. Comparative and observational work is needed to examine whether mutual witnessing supports learning and under what conditions AI changes it.

# Conclusion

In this member-governed makerspace, participants associated continued community life with formal arrangements, socially earned authority, and the visibility of individually pursued work. They described AI as useful for some bounded tasks while retaining responsibility for checking and understanding its outputs. The case makes a learning-sciences question visible: how can voluntary spaces sustain the practices through which members show work, recognise competence, and learn with one another as new tools enter those practices?
